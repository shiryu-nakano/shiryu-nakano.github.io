**1 Introduction**

**(1)** Over-parameterized neural networks can represent an enormous number of functions that fit their training data (Zhang et al., 2021), yet training does not select among them arbitrarily. It favors simple ones. This preference is one of the main handles we have on why highly expressive networks learn structured solutions rather than arbitrary ones. What is not settled is what "simple" refers to.
→ここの文章がわかりずらい，さっきのランダムなラベルに適合するほど表現力が高いとか訓練損失0でテストではランダム推論するようにできるほど表現力が高いとかここをシンプルに言った方がいい


**(2)** Two lines of work answer differently, and on different objects. The simplicity bias (SB) describes a hierarchy in the learned *function*: networks trained with stochastic gradient descent fit simple classifiers before more complex, non-linear ones (Kalimeris et al., 2019; Rahaman et al., 2019; Valle-Pérez et al., 2019). The distributional simplicity bias (DSB) describes a hierarchy in the *data* a network exploits: low-order statistics such as the mean and covariance before higher-order ones such as (co)skewness and (co)kurtosis (Refinetti et al., 2023; Belrose et al., 2024; Rende et al., 2024). Both report the same ordering, simple before complex, but one on the function side and one on the data side.
Whether they are two faces of one principle or two unrelated regularities is unknown.直接結び付けられていない
→ここはいい感じ

**(3)** The question decides what simplicity bias is. If the two hierarchies correspond, then simplicity bias is not a property of function space alone. It is a link from the statistical structure of the training data to the complexity of what the network ends up learning, and the preference for simple solutions becomes something the data can help explain, rather than something attributed to the architecture or the optimizer alone (Neyshabur et al., 2015; Valle-Pérez et al., 2019). Establishing such a link means treating data, architecture and learning dynamics as one interacting system (Zdeborová, 2020; Bahri et al., 2020; Simon et al., 2026).
→→3．4のいちづけ，役割が微妙にわかりずらい，なんでここを入れるの？


**(4)** Testing the correspondence needs two things. The first is a way to vary data complexity under control, and DSB supplies it: the construction used to establish DSB builds synthetic datasets whose class-conditional moments match those of a real dataset up to a specified order $k$. Seen as a phenomenon, DSB is a claim about learning dynamics. Seen as a tool, it turns data complexity into a variable that can be raised one well-defined step at a time. The second is a way to measure the complexity of what is learned, and this is missing. SB and DSB are both read off behavior, from the loss or the accuracy of a network over the course of training. That describes what a network does, not what it becomes: the weights it converges to, and the local geometry of the loss landscape around them. Work that studies trained weights directly (Unterthiner et al., 2020; Eilertsen et al., 2020; Martin et al., 2021; Schürholt et al., 2021; Haim et al., 2022) does not vary the training data under control, and so cannot say what in a solution came from the data.

**(5)** For the second we turn to singular learning theory (Watanabe, 2009). Deep networks are singular models: the loss landscape around a solution is degenerate, so complexity measures built for regular models do not carry over. A parameter count reflects effective complexity only when a model is regular, and Hessian-based flatness is sensitive to degeneracy and to the choice of parameterization (Wei et al., 2022). We use the local learning coefficient (LLC; Lau et al., 2025), which quantifies that degeneracy around a learned solution and is invariant to parameter symmetries by construction. It measures local geometric complexity, defined on the solution rather than on the network's behavior. With a data-side dial and a solution-side measure in hand, we ask:

1. Does the moment-order hierarchy of the training data manifest as an ordered hierarchy in the local geometric complexity of the learned solutions?
2. To what extent is this relationship architecture-dependent?

**(6)** We construct datasets whose class-conditional moments match those of real CIFAR-10 up to order $k = 1, \dots, 6$, train three architectures on each, and estimate the LLC of every converged solution. The three are two convolutional networks and a vision transformer of comparable parameter count, and the training recipe is fixed across all conditions. Absolute LLC values are not comparable across architectures, so we normalize each curve by that architecture's own real-data value. This lets us ask whether the dependence of solution complexity on $k$ separates into an architecture-dependent scale and a data-dependent profile.

**(7)** This is one half of the correspondence. We measure the complexity of learned solutions, not of learned functions, so we test the data-to-solution link and leave the function side, and with it a direct test of SB, to future work. Our design is also cross-sectional: we train a separate model for each $k$ and compare endpoints rather than tracking one run over time, so we do not address training dynamics. Our conclusions are drawn from one dataset family and three architectures.

#+begin_export latex

Our results suggest that the data-side hierarchy identified by DSB has a corresponding hierarchy in the geometry of learned solutions. As progressively higher-order statistics are restored to the training data, the LLC of the resulting solutions also increases. This provides a measurable solution-side link between the statistical hierarchy of DSB and the functional hierarchy studied under SB, while stopping short of establishing a direct correspondence between the two. We next discuss what this result implies, how it depends on architecture and measurement, and the limitations of our experimental design.

* Intro final
The local learning coefficient (LLC)~\citep{lau2025local}, 
grounded in singular learning theory (SLT)~\citep{watanabe2009algebraic}, 
is the appropriate tool for studying the relationship between architecture and data.
This relationship appears in the loss landscape, and optimization is a trajectory on this landscape.
Measuring the shape of the landscape therefore means probing how architecture and data interact.
Deep neural networks are singular models, whose loss landscape is degenerate around a solution.
Measures such as parameter counting or Hessian-based flatness cannot capture this degeneracy~\citep{wei2022deep}.
SLT provides a framework for handling such degeneracy.
The LLC characterizes the local geometry around a degenerate solution, and is therefore the right tool for studying the relationship between architecture and data.

** P2：DSB——単純性バイアスの操作可能な定式化
*** TS：単純性バイアスの中でも、Refinetti らの distributional simplicity bias（DSB）は、バイアスを実験的に操作可能な形で定式化した点で際立っている [Refinetti et al. 2023]。

** p1
The ability of deep neural networks to simultaneously fit their training data and generalize well has been explained by various simplicity biases [Kalimeris et al., 2019, Rahaman et al., 2019, Valle-Pérez et al., 2019].
These theories postulate that networks trained with stochastic gradient descent learn simple structure first and increase complexity only as far as needed to fit the data.
This bias would prevent the network from memorizing the training set, even though the training loss has global minima with high generalization error [Liu et al., 2020].

** p2

The distributional simplicity bias (DSB) places simplicity in the statistical structure of the training data[Refinetti et al., 2023, Belrose et al., 2024]: networks exploit low-order moments, such as the mean and covariance, before learning to use its higher-order statistics. 
Belrose et al. [2024] construct synthetic datasets whose class-conditional moments match those of real data up to a specified order k, and examine model behavior along this hierarchy. 
DSB thus shifts the focus of simplicity biases from characterizing the function that the network learns to identifying the features of the training data that influence learning.


** p3

However, it has not been measured how the learned solution changes as the statistical structure of the training data is varied order by order. What prior studies observe is model behavior—the accuracy of models trained or evaluated on moment-matched data—and as a test of which statistics networks exploit, this is complete. Yet what simplicity biases were meant to explain is the selection of solutions, and their account says that a solution grows only as complex as the data demands. Since DSB grades this demand by the moment order k, the account, if correct, predicts that the complexity of learned solutions should increase step by step with k. Whether this solution-side gradation exists—and if so, what form it takes—is an open question.
